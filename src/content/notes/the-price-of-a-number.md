# The Price of a Number

## Lp(a), the commerce of risk, and why I refuse to confuse a measurement with a human being

I came to this question in the most ordinary way possible: I read a
claim about a blood marker and asked what, precisely, the claim meant.

Not what the headline meant. Not what a pharmaceutical company might
eventually sell for it. Not what a guideline committee had decided to
call "high". I wanted to know what the thing actually was, where it came
from, what made it change, and---most importantly---whether changing it
changed the fate of the person carrying it.

That should not be a rebellious way to approach medicine.

Yet increasingly it feels like one.

The marker was lipoprotein(a), or Lp(a). I was told the familiar version
of the story. It is genetic. It is largely fixed for life. About one
person in five has a high level. It is considerably more atherogenic
than ordinary LDL on a per-particle basis. Most conventional treatments
barely touch it. New drugs can lower it spectacularly.

There is enough truth in that paragraph to make it persuasive.

There is also enough compression in it to make me uncomfortable.

I work with blood. I look at people, not merely reference ranges. My
instinct is therefore to ask the awkward second question: **what are we
actually observing?**

That question matters because modern medicine has developed a peculiar
habit of allowing a measurement to acquire the authority of the disease
it is supposed to help us understand.

A number is measured.

The number correlates with an outcome.

A threshold is established.

The threshold becomes a risk category.

The risk category becomes a clinical problem.

The clinical problem becomes a therapeutic target.

Then, almost inevitably, somebody discovers how to sell the movement of
the number.

At no point in that chain need anybody be corrupt. That is what makes
the problem more interesting.

An incentive structure does not require a conspiracy.

It merely requires incentives.

## I do not dispute that Lp(a) is real

Let me make my position difficult to misrepresent.

Lp(a) exists. Elevated concentrations are associated with cardiovascular
disease. The genetic evidence implicating the **LPA** locus is
substantial. I am not proposing that genes are imaginary, that
cardiovascular disease is imaginary, or that every cardiologist has been
bought by a pharmaceutical company.

Those would be lazy arguments.

My objection is to the leap from **"this is associated with risk"** to
**"this number is destiny"**, and then from **"this number is a risk
marker"** to **"lowering this number must necessarily improve the
outcome that matters."**

Those are different propositions.

They require different evidence.

The distinction is not pedantry. It is the whole argument.

## "Fixed for life" is not what biology looks like

The phrase that first caught my attention was that Lp(a) is genetically
determined and essentially fixed for life.

Then I looked at the evidence.

Serial measurements do not always behave as that slogan suggests.

A 2025 analysis of placebo-treated participants in OCEAN(a)-DOSE
reported intra-individual variation of about 10%. A quarter of
participants had at least one excursion of 25% or more from their
individual mean, and more than half had an absolute excursion of at
least 50 nmol/L at one or more visits.[^1]

A 2024 study of 609 people found changes greater than 25% in 40.5% of
participants. Among those initially sitting in the intermediate "grey
zone", more than half changed risk category after another
measurement.[^2]

A 2026 real-world study of 992 patients reported a median relative
change of 14.4%, with 11.3% crossing a risk-category boundary.[^3]

Even seasonality has been reported. An older study found higher mean
Lp(a) concentrations during colder months than warmer months.[^4]

This does **not** prove that Lp(a) is useless. It does not prove that
weather is its master. It does not disprove its inherited component.

It proves something much simpler:

**the biology is more complicated than the slogan.**

And that is exactly where I become interested.

There is counter-evidence too. A carefully controlled study using paired
samples measured on the same assay plate found extremely high long-term
correlation and reported that 91.6% of participants remained in the same
clinical category.[^5]

Good.

I want that evidence as well.

I have no interest in constructing an alternative orthodoxy in which I
simply throw away every result that inconveniences me.

My conclusion is therefore not that Lp(a) "changes with the weather" and
is worthless. My conclusion is that **a generally stable biomarker can
still show meaningful biological and analytical variability,
particularly around thresholds where medicine suddenly changes what it
calls a person.**

That is a much less marketable sentence.

It is also closer to the truth.

## Then I asked the obvious question: what about food?

Here the conversation becomes even stranger.

We are told that Lp(a) is overwhelmingly genetic and that lifestyle has
comparatively little influence upon it.

Again, the first half contains important truth. The **LPA** gene,
including variation in its kringle IV type-2 repeat structure, strongly
influences circulating Lp(a). I do not deny this.

But I reject the habit of using the word *genetic* as though it meant
*metabolically isolated*.

The liver is not a hard drive reading an immutable file.

It is living tissue.

It receives substrate. It responds to hormones. It responds to energy
balance, inflammation, endocrine state, disease, medication and the
biochemical environment in which those genes are expressed.

Genes matter.

Environment matters.

The interesting question is the interaction.

And food does, in fact, move Lp(a).

A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis of 27 randomised controlled
dietary trials found that diets lower in saturated fat produced modestly
**higher**, not lower, Lp(a) than diets containing more saturated fat.
Replacing saturated fat with carbohydrate was associated with higher
Lp(a).[^6]

That finding alone ought to make anyone suspicious of cartoon nutrition.

A randomised controlled feeding trial went further. After twenty weeks,
its low-carbohydrate diet---the diet containing the most saturated
fat---reduced Lp(a) by 14.7%, while the moderate- and high-carbohydrate
diets did not produce a comparable reduction.[^7]

The European Atherosclerosis Society's own consensus statement
acknowledges dietary and physiological modulation, including the
observation that replacing saturated fat with carbohydrate or
unsaturated fat may increase Lp(a), while a low-carbohydrate diet high
in saturated fat may decrease it.[^8]

None of this establishes my strongest suspicion---that the metabolic
environment may matter far more than present orthodoxy allows.

But neither does it permit the lazy statement that food is irrelevant.

This is where I part company with both tribes.

I will not pretend that genetic architecture disappears because I
believe food matters.

Nor will I pretend that metabolism disappears because somebody has
calculated high heritability.

**"Genetic" and "environmental" are not opposing religions.**

They are parts of the same organism.

## Six times more dangerous

Then comes the phrase designed for repetition:

**Lp(a) is approximately six times more atherogenic than LDL.**

Read the smaller print.

The 2024 genetic analysis estimated Lp(a) to be about 6.6 times as
atherogenic **per particle** as LDL.[^9]

Those final two words matter enormously.

The study did not discover that a person with elevated Lp(a) is
automatically 6.6 times more likely to have a heart attack. It estimated
relative atherogenicity per apoB-containing particle using genetic
analysis.

An accompanying editorial supplied the context that disappears in
popular retelling: LDL particles are generally much more numerous.[^10]

So a sophisticated result becomes a frightening slogan simply by
removing its denominator.

This is not necessarily dishonesty.

It is something more commonplace.

It is what happens when nuance travels through institutions that reward
certainty.

## My problem is the surrogate

This is the part I care about most.

Suppose tomorrow somebody gives me a tablet that lowers Lp(a) by 90%.

What has been proved?

That the tablet lowers Lp(a) by 90%.

That is all.

If I am interested in human health rather than laboratory aesthetics, I
then have another question:

**What happened to the person?**

Did fewer people have myocardial infarctions?

Did fewer have strokes?

Did fewer die?

Did they live longer?

Did they live better?

What adverse effects appeared?

What was the absolute benefit?

How many people had to be treated for one person to benefit?

Those questions seem almost embarrassingly obvious. Yet medicine has
repeatedly learnt that improving a surrogate endpoint does not guarantee
improvement in the clinical outcome presumed to sit behind it.

A review of 220 cardiovascular trials using primary surrogate endpoints
found that many interventions succeeded on their surrogate while
subsequent clinical-outcome trials failed to confirm the hoped-for
benefit.[^11]

The history of CETP inhibitors provides another warning: impressive
changes in lipid biomarkers did not reliably translate into the expected
clinical success.[^12]

This is why I refuse to be dazzled by percentages attached to
biomarkers.

Ninety per cent.

Ninety-five per cent.

Ninety-nine per cent.

Wonderful.

Now tell me what happened to the human beings.

## And now, conveniently, we can lower it

Muvalaplin, an oral small molecule, has produced placebo-adjusted
reductions in Lp(a) approaching 86% in a phase II trial.[^13]

Other experimental agents have produced similarly dramatic biochemical
effects.

This is scientifically interesting. It may become clinically important.
I hope that if these treatments genuinely prevent heart attacks, strokes
and deaths with acceptable harms, the trials demonstrate it clearly.

I have no ideological need for the drugs to fail.

That would make me no better than the people I criticise.

But I equally have no obligation to pretend that lowering a marker is
identical to improving an outcome before the experiment has established
it.

At the time of writing, the pivotal Lp(a)HORIZON trial of pelacarsen has
been designed precisely to answer the important question: whether
lowering Lp(a) reduces major cardiovascular events in people with
established cardiovascular disease and elevated Lp(a).[^14]

Until the outcome evidence answers that question, a dramatic fall in
Lp(a) remains exactly what it is:

**a dramatic fall in Lp(a).**

It is not a heart attack prevented.

It is not a life extended.

It is not a death avoided.

Not yet.

## I do not need a conspiracy. The business model is enough.

This is where people become nervous.

Mention money in medicine and one is immediately sorted into one of two
camps.

Either pharmaceutical companies are satanic organisations manufacturing
fictitious diseases, or they are merely benevolent scientific
institutions that happen, by extraordinary coincidence, to have
shareholders.

I believe neither.

A pharmaceutical company is a company.

Its purpose is to develop products that can be sold profitably.

There is nothing mysterious about this.

What is mysterious is the expectation that enormous financial incentives
should have no influence whatsoever upon the scientific ecosystem
surrounding those products.

Which questions receive funding?

Which endpoints become fashionable?

Which researchers build careers around them?

Which conferences discuss them?

Which guidelines incorporate them?

Which findings are published?

Which failures disappear quietly?

Which risk thresholds enlarge a potential treatment population?

These are not conspiracy questions.

They are systems questions.

And systems respond to incentives.

A systematic review covering 75 studies found industry-sponsored drug
and device research more likely than non-industry-sponsored research to
report favourable efficacy results and favourable conclusions.[^15]

A *BMJ* systematic review found financial conflicts of interest
associated overall with more favourable recommendations concerning drugs
and devices in guidelines, advisory reports, opinion pieces and
narrative reviews.[^16]

A 2024 Cochrane review following more than 165,000 clinical trials found
that only about 53% were published in full, with positive trials more
likely to reach full publication than negative or null trials.[^17]

None of those findings allows me to point at a particular scientist and
call him corrupt.

I do not need to.

They demonstrate something more useful:

**the literature is produced inside a human economic system, and that
system leaves fingerprints on the literature.**

Peer review does not abolish incentives.

A university appointment does not abolish incentives.

A disclosure form does not abolish incentives.

Nor does the existence of an incentive automatically make the result
false.

It means I read more carefully.

That is all.

## This is personal for me

I have spent enough of my working life around real systems to distrust
explanations that survive only because nobody walks out to the machine
and checks what it is actually doing.

In instrumentation and automation, a transmitter can give you a
beautiful number while the process itself is going wrong.

A calibrated instrument is not the process.

A dashboard is not the plant.

A model is not the machine.

And a laboratory marker is not the human being.

That lesson has followed me into my work with blood.

When I look at blood, I cannot forget that it came from a person who ate
something, slept or did not sleep, moved or did not move, experienced
stress, took medication, lived in an environment, possesses a history
and exists as an integrated biological system.

The number is evidence about that system.

It is not the system itself.

This is perhaps why I am instinctively resistant when medicine asks me
to stop at the number.

I want to know what produced it.

I want to know what else changed with it.

I want to know whether it changes when the environment changes.

I want to know whether correcting the supposed abnormality improves the
person.

And if the answer is *we do not know*, I want those exact words.

Not a euphemism.

Not a guideline-shaped certainty.

Not a sales forecast dressed as inevitability.

**We do not know.**

There is tremendous intellectual dignity in that sentence.

## Health is finite. Risk is infinite.

There is another reason I think this matters.

Disease places a natural limit on a medical market.

Risk does not.

A person either has a fractured femur or does not.

But a healthy person can possess an almost unlimited number of
probabilities concerning diseases that might happen in the future.

Measure enough things and everyone becomes abnormal somewhere.

Create enough thresholds and everyone becomes a patient eventually.

Again, risk-factor medicine is not inherently fraudulent. Treating
hypertension prevents real disease. Stopping smoking prevents real
disease. Prevention can be one of medicine's greatest achievements.

But the economics deserve scrutiny precisely because the potential
market is enormous.

Once a measurable variable is linked to future disease, an ecosystem can
form around it:

testing;

thresholds;

risk calculators;

specialists;

repeat testing;

guidelines;

treatment;

monitoring;

and eventually lifelong customers.

Sometimes that ecosystem saves lives.

Sometimes it becomes expensive numerical housekeeping.

The only protection against confusing the two is outcome evidence.

## I have nothing to sell you

This matters enough that I want to state it plainly.

There is no supplement at the bottom of this page.

There is no consultation package hidden behind the argument.

There is no proprietary Lp(a) protocol.

There is no affiliate link.

There is no pharmaceutical sponsor.

There is no requirement that you adopt my diet.

There is not even a requirement that you agree with me.

I am not asking for money in exchange for the truth.

I am asking you to inspect the reasoning.

If an Lp(a)-lowering drug eventually demonstrates a substantial
reduction in heart attacks, strokes or cardiovascular deaths, with
acceptable harms and meaningful absolute benefit, I will say so.

If it lowers the laboratory value magnificently while producing little
meaningful improvement in human outcomes, I will say that too.

If rigorous dietary research eventually demonstrates that my suspicions
about metabolism are wrong, I will change my position.

If it demonstrates that they are right, I expect conventional medicine
to do the same.

That is the bargain I want with reality.

**I owe no loyalty to an opinion merely because it was mine yesterday.**

That, to me, is the difference between pursuing truth and defending a
position.

## The question I keep coming back to

After all the papers, the thresholds, the genetic analyses, the trials
and the extraordinary technology, I return to an almost primitive
question:

**What happened to the human being?**

Not:

Did the number fall?

Not:

Did the confidence interval exclude one?

Not:

Did the biomarker reach its prespecified endpoint?

Not:

Did the mechanism make sense?

Not:

Did the guideline committee vote for it?

Not:

Can the intervention be patented?

What happened to the human being?

Modern medicine has achieved extraordinary things when it has answered
that question properly. I am alive in a civilisation immeasurably
improved by anaesthesia, antibiotics, trauma surgery, emergency
medicine, imaging, sterile technique and countless other achievements of
scientific medicine.

Those achievements deserve respect.

They do not purchase immunity from future questioning.

Authority must be earned with each proposition.

The white coat is not evidence.

The university crest is not evidence.

The impact factor is not evidence.

The guideline is not evidence.

The pharmaceutical prospectus is certainly not evidence.

Each may point towards evidence.

None is reality itself.

Reality is gloriously indifferent to reputation.

A molecule behaves as it behaves.

A body responds as it responds.

A treatment either improves meaningful outcomes or it does not.

And a dead man cannot be revived by pointing out that his surrogate
endpoint improved beautifully.

So I am interested in Lp(a), but not because I have already decided what
the answer must be.

I am interested because it illustrates something much larger about
modern medicine.

An observation becomes an association.

The association becomes a risk.

The risk becomes a threshold.

The threshold becomes a target.

The target becomes a drug.

The drug becomes a market.

And somewhere during that journey, it becomes remarkably easy to forget
the question with which medicine ought to have begun:

**Does doing this actually make the person live longer or live better?**

Measure Lp(a).

Study it.

Challenge it.

Study the genetics.

Study the food.

Study the liver.

Study the environment.

Build the drugs.

Run the trials.

Publish the failures.

Declare the conflicts.

Show the raw outcomes.

And when the evidence contradicts the theory, discard the theory.

That is not hostility to science.

That is science.

What I reject is the strange modern substitute in which uncertainty is
embarrassing, consensus becomes authority, association becomes destiny
and the movement of a laboratory number is allowed to stand in for the
restoration of human health.

Truth does not require a subscription.

Logic has no shareholders.

And reality does not care who funded the study.
